Over the past few days, Ledger has confirmed incidents involving compromised hardware wallets purchased through an authorized reseller, resulting in users losing funds.

Calling these events unfortunate and disappointing would be an understatement.

Here's where we at Walletbeat stand, why we remain optimistic despite these setbacks, and how we believe wallet security can improve moving forward.

It is worth mentioning that Walletbeat has not yet covered the topic of hardware wallets fully as the focus have been on software wallets.

What happened?

On October 9, Ledger announced that it was investigating reports of users in Southeast Asia losing funds after purchasing hardware wallets from CryptoBilis, an authorized Ledger reseller for Malaysia, Indonesia, and Philippines. Findings suggested that the implant captures recovery phrases and transmit them through cellular activity. Even if your hardware wallet is secure through strong cryptography, secure element, and legitimate firmware, it can still be vulnerable if an attacker can physically modify the device to observe sensitive information.

What makes this incident especially concerning is that the affected devices were distributed through an authorized reseller. Users followed a commonly recommended security practice: purchase from recognized sellers than unknown third parties. Yet this was still not sufficient to protect them.

This raises important questions about supply-chain security, physical tamper resistance and the extent to which users can independently verify the integrity of their devices.

Where does Walletbeat stand?

We believe wallet security should not be reduced to individual features, certifications, or marketing claims. Security is a combination of different properties, each addressing different threats and requiring different assumptions.

Open-source firmware, for example, makes it possible for independent researchers to inspect how a wallet operates. Reproducible builds can help establish whether distributed software corresponds to publicly available source code. Hardware security mechanisms can protect private keys against certain forms of extraction or unauthorized access.

But none of these properties, individually, guarantees that a device has not been physically tampered with. A wallet can satisfy one security property while remaining vulnerable in another.

We believe it is important to evaluate security properties independently, rather than treating a wallet as simply secure or insecure.

Why we ramin optimistic

Despite what happened, we remain optimistic about the future of wallet security. 

While there may have been better ways to protect the users in what have happened, we should also recognize that security is not a solved problem. There will always be new attack vectors, new vulnerabilities, and new challenges as wallets, and technology continues to evolve.

The work of independent security researchers, affected users sharing their experiecnes, and the broader community investigating these incidents is incredibly important. It allows previously overlooked threats to become better understood. What matters is how the ecosystem responds to them. Rather than treating inidents like these as reasons to self-custody, we believe they should motivate the ecosystem to improve the tools and protections that make self-custody possible.

Moving forward

In @VitalikButerin's reent article, The Importance of Full-Stack Openness and Verifiability, Vitalik emphasizes the importance of building technology that is ont only open-source, but also independently verifiable, across bothe software and hardware.

"No software is secure if you can't be sure that your hardware is actually running that software, and is not leaking data separately on the side."

We should strive toward a future where users don't have to rely solely on trusting manufactures, authorized resellers, and third party certifications to know if the devices are secure. we should reduce trust assumptions through openness and verifiability.

The goal should not simply convince users that their wallets are secure, but make it increasingly possible for them to independently verify that they are. 

While this is still a long way to go, we believe this is a future worth working toward.
